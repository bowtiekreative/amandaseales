**Logline:** An autistic creator defends a celebrity against the internet — gets blocked by her for naming how she talks about autism — and discovers the block is the tip of a mechanism the research never fully named: the elite capture of a diagnosis.

**Runtime target:** 60 min · 12 chapters · ~32 min narration (AI VO @ ~150 wpm) + ~25 min A-roll clips + ~3 min infographic beats.

- `[CLIP]` — cut from your video files. `File` = the video matching that `.srt` name in the repo. Timecodes are from the SRT (caption-accurate; pad ±2s when cutting).

# CHAPTER 6 — TWENTY YEARS OF WARNINGS (~6 min) — *the research chapter*

**[NARRATION 6.1]**
Everything I've shown you has been documented by researchers — piece by piece — for over twenty years.
Two thousand three. Mark Deal, in the journal Disability and Society, documents what he calls a hierarchy of impairments. Disabled people ranking other disabled people. Distancing themselves from the "wrong kind" of disabled. Competing for scarce resources and respectability.

**[SCREENSHOT 2]** — Deal, M. (2003). "Disabled people's attitudes toward other impairment groups: a hierarchy of impairments." *Disability & Society*, 18(7), 897–910. DOI: 10.1080/0968759032000127317 — screenshot the title/abstract page at tandfonline.com.

**[NARRATION 6.2]**
Two thousand ten. An autistic blogger named Mel Baggs writes an essay with a title that should have ended this conversation forever: "Aspie supremacy can kill."
Baggs — a non-speaking autistic person — warned that when "smart aspies" separate themselves from the rest of us, the people left below the line pay the price.

**[SCREENSHOT 3]** — Mel Baggs, "Aspie supremacy can kill" (2010), ballastexistenz blog — screenshot the archived post (search "Baggs aspie supremacy can kill" — use the Wayback Machine capture).

**[NARRATION 6.3]**
Two thousand nineteen. Researcher Anna de Hooge, in Disability Studies Quarterly, publishes "Binary Boys: Autism, Aspie Supremacy and Post/Humanist Normativity."
De Hooge shows the aspie-supremacist archetype is always drawn the same way — superior because intelligent, superior because productive, superior because economically worthy.

**[SCREENSHOT 4]** — de Hooge, A. (2019). "Binary Boys: Autism, Aspie Supremacy and Post/Humanist Normativity." *Disability Studies Quarterly*, 39(1). DOI: 10.18061/dsq.v39i1.6461 — free full text at dsq-sds.org; screenshot title + abstract.

**[NARRATION 6.4]**
Twenty twenty-five. Patrick Dwyer and colleagues, in the journal Autism, describe "neurodiversity-lite": performatively celebrating neurodivergence while stereotyping neurodivergent people as having marketable superpowers.
Superpowers. Remember "the supertism"?

**[SCREENSHOT 5]** — Dwyer, P., Gurba, A. N., Kapp, S. K., et al. (2025). "Community views of neurodiversity, models of disability and autism intervention." *Autism* (SAGE). DOI: 10.1177/13623613241273029 — screenshot title page + the "neurodiversity lite" passage.

**[NARRATION 6.5]**
Hierarchy of impairment. Aspie supremacy. Neurodiversity-lite.
Three names, three decades, one pattern.
But here's what I realized, sitting with my block, reading these papers.
The literature describes the surface — what it looks like. It never fully names the machine underneath — how it runs, and why it keeps running through people who genuinely love their community.
For that, we need one more book. And it's not about autism at all.

# CHAPTER 7 — ELITE CAPTURE (~5 min)

**[NARRATION 7.1]**
Twenty twenty-two. Philosopher Olúfẹ́mi Táíwò publishes "Elite Capture: How the Powerful Took Over Identity Politics."

**[SCREENSHOT 6]** — Táíwò, O. O. (2022). *Elite Capture*. Haymarket Books — screenshot the cover + a passage defining elite capture.

**[NARRATION 7.2]**
Táíwò's argument, in one line: whenever a group holds something valuable in common — a movement, a language, an identity — the most advantaged members of that group tend to end up controlling it, and speaking in its name.
Not because they're villains. Because they have the capital — money, fame, platform, fluency — and nobody audits them.
Now apply that to autism.
What does the autistic community hold in common? The category itself. The word. Who it covers. What it means. What it demands from the world.
That is a public good. And a public good without a gatekeeper is exactly what Táíwò says gets captured.
Here's my contribution — the piece I'm adding to the literature.
Autism, today, is what I call a low-audit category. And that is precisely what makes it capturable.
