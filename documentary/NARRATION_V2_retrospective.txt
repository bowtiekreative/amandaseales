Amanda Seales is one of the most recognizable multi-hyphenates of her generation. A comedian, an actress, a singer, a poet, an author, and in recent years, one of the most prominent political commentators on social media. In 2024, sitting across from Shannon Sharpe on Club Shay Shay, she shared something that would reshape how a lot of people understood her: she was autistic. For thousands of late-diagnosed autistic adults, and especially for Black autistic women, that moment meant being seen on one of the biggest platforms in culture. But in the years since, the way Amanda Seales talks about autism has become a story of its own. A story that raises a question that goes well beyond her. And that question is what this video is about. I should mention up front that I have a small personal part in this story. We'll get to it later, when it becomes relevant. But before we can talk about how Amanda Seales talks about autism, we have to understand who she is, and how she got here.

There's something I want to be clear about from the beginning, because videos like this one are easily mistaken for something they're not. This is not a takedown. When the internet turned on Amanda Seales, I made a video defending her. I still stand by that video. What follows is closer to a question I couldn't put down: what happens when the most visible autistic person in a community starts describing autism in a way the research has been warning about for twenty years? It turns out the answer is complicated. And to understand it, you'll need to know a little about who's asking.

My name is Ryan Perez. I'm autistic, dyslexic, and I have ADHD, and like Amanda, I wasn't diagnosed until well into adulthood. I've spent about twenty years building digital systems, and the past several making content about neurodivergent life under the name Digital Stem Cell. I mention all of this not to establish credentials, but because this video is a conversation happening inside the autistic community, not outside of it. The critiques you're going to hear were first made by autistic people, about how autism gets represented to the world. And as we'll see, how a community gets represented, and by whom, turns out to matter a great deal.

To talk about Amanda Seales and autism, it helps to start where she starts: with the diagnosis itself, and what it meant to her. Seales grew up in Orlando, Florida, a Black girl who was told, constantly, that she talked white. She has a poem about it, written when she was nineteen, called Oreo.

Anyone who has been through a late diagnosis will recognize what she's describing. The decades of masking. The vocabulary consciously rearranged to fit in. The exhausting work of performing a self that other people would accept. And when the realization finally came, listen to what it gave her.

It's worth sitting with how genuinely moving this is. The relief of finally having an explanation. The shame that lifts when you learn you were never broken, just different. This is the story of millions of late-diagnosed adults, and when Amanda Seales tells it, she tells it well. Whatever else this video has to say, that part of her story deserves to be taken seriously, because everything that follows grows out of it. And that, I think, is what makes the next part so difficult to see clearly. The trouble doesn't begin where the story turns false. It begins where a true story starts being used for something else.

Somewhere along the way, the framing changed. Autism, in Amanda's telling, stopped being an explanation and started becoming a kind of credential. Perhaps the clearest example is how she responds to people who question her self-diagnosis. Rather than pointing to diagnostic criteria, or to the clinicians she says guided her through testing, she points to something else entirely: her talent.

Now, being multi-talented is not a diagnostic criterion for autism. It never has been, and most autistic people are not celebrated multi-hyphenates, nor are they any less autistic for it. But notice what this argument does. It doesn't merely describe Amanda Seales. It quietly redefines what autism is, with exceptional ability as the proof of membership. And talent isn't where it stops. Across her live streams, the theme repeats with remarkable consistency: genius, output, IQ. There's even a word for it that surfaced on one of her streams, offered by a fan and repeated by Amanda with evident approval: the supertism. Intelligence. Innovation. Productivity. It's worth pausing on what those three measures actually are, because they are not neutral. They are the exact measuring sticks that ableism has always used to decide which people count. And in these moments, they are being imported, wholesale, into the definition of autism.

Here is the thing about importing a ranking system: the moment you install one, someone has to rank below. And this is where Amanda's framing of autism goes somewhere the research has a name for. In one live stream, discussing the difference between neurotypical and neurodivergent minds, she describes neurotypical people, which is to say, most of the human race, as controlled minds. As clones. And she goes further, describing a planet composed of what she calls drones and individuals, without quite rejecting the idea that the drones were somehow made that way. In another stream, she explains that the reason she was able to escape indoctrination, when her audience could not, is that her brain is wired in a way that makes indoctrination difficult. Taken one clip at a time, these can each be waved away as a colorful turn of phrase. Taken together, a structure comes into view. Autistic means talented, brilliant, resistant to manipulation. Neurotypical means drone. There is a name for the belief that one neurotype is superior to another. The autistic community coined it themselves, as a warning, more than fifteen years ago. It's called aspie supremacy. And the history of that warning is worth taking seriously, because none of this is new.

In 2003, researcher Mark Deal published a paper in the journal Disability and Society titled "Disabled people's attitudes toward other impairment groups." In it, Deal documented something uncomfortable: a hierarchy of impairments, in which disabled people themselves rank other disabled people, distancing themselves from what they perceive as the wrong kind of disabled. It wasn't a paper about autism specifically. It was a paper about a pattern.

In 2010, an autistic blogger named Mel Baggs, who was themselves non-speaking, gave that pattern its sharpest name in an essay titled "Aspie supremacy can kill." Baggs warned that when so-called smart aspies set themselves apart from, and above, the rest of the autistic community, it is the people at the bottom of that ranking who pay the price. The title was not a metaphor. Baggs meant it literally.

In 2019, researcher Anna de Hooge published "Binary Boys: Autism, Aspie Supremacy and Post-Humanist Normativity" in Disability Studies Quarterly. De Hooge examined how the aspie supremacist archetype is always constructed the same way: superior because intelligent, superior because productive, superior because economically worthy. If those measures sound familiar, they should.

And in 2025, Patrick Dwyer and his colleagues, writing in the journal Autism, described a phenomenon they called neurodiversity-lite: a way of celebrating neurodivergence that stereotypes neurodivergent people as having marketable superpowers, while quietly leaving the disability part of the story behind. Superpowers. It's hard to hear that word and not think of the supertism.

So the pattern has been documented, named, and renamed, across more than twenty years of scholarship. A hierarchy of impairment. Aspie supremacy. Neurodiversity-lite. Which raises an obvious question: if the warnings have been on the record this whole time, why does the pattern keep happening? Why does it keep appearing in people who, by every indication, genuinely love their community? The literature describes what this looks like. What it doesn't fully explain is the machine underneath, how it runs, and why it keeps running. For that, we need one more book. And interestingly, it isn't about autism at all.

In 2022, the philosopher Olúfẹ́mi Táíwò published a book called Elite Capture: How the Powerful Took Over Identity Politics. Táíwò's argument, reduced to a sentence, goes like this: whenever a group holds something valuable in common, a movement, a language, an identity, the most advantaged members of that group will tend, over time, to end up controlling it and speaking in its name. Not because they are villains, but because they have the resources, the fame, the platform, the fluency, and because, crucially, nobody is in a position to check them. Now apply that to autism. What does the autistic community hold in common? The category itself. The word. What it means, who it covers, and what it asks of the world. That is a commons, in every meaningful sense. And a commons with no gatekeeper is precisely what Táíwò tells us gets captured. Which brings us to the piece of this story I haven't seen anyone else lay out, and the reason I think autism is uniquely vulnerable to what we've been watching.

Autism, today, is what you might call a low-audit category. What I mean by that is simple: the community has collectively agreed, for good reasons, that you do not get to demand someone's diagnostic paperwork. Adult assessment can cost thousands of dollars. Amanda has cited a figure of ten thousand, and while it varies, the barrier is real. And the research is unambiguous that Black women in particular are chronically misdiagnosed, dismissed, and diagnosed late, if at all. Self-identification, for many people, is the only door that exists. On all of this, Amanda Seales is simply right, and it needs to be said plainly. Suspending the audit was the compassionate choice, and I am not here to argue for gatekeeping the most vulnerable people in my community. But every design choice has a side effect, and this one is worth naming carefully. A category no one is permitted to audit is also a category that anyone with enough capital and enough charisma can walk into, redefine, and eventually run. The same open door admits two very different people: the poor autistic person who could never afford an assessment, and the celebrity who enters on talent and vibes, and then re-centers the entire category on herself. The justice of the open door is real. So is its vulnerability. They are the same door.

There is a way to tell whether a category has been captured, and it isn't by checking anyone's diagnosis. You watch who is allowed to ask questions. When the gossip site Baller Alert reposted Amanda's own words about not being clinically diagnosed, her response was not clarification. It was a ruling. Her diagnosis, she said, was not up for discussion, and anyone questioning a non-clinical diagnosis was, by definition, revealing that they know nothing about autism. Consider the shape of that rule for a moment. The only people it disqualifies from asking questions are the people asking questions. She entered the category without an audit, which, as we've established, the community's open door permits. But she now also administers the audit, deciding which questions are legitimate and which questioners are ignorant, or bots, or what she calls ops. In Táíwò's framework, this is what capture looks like from the inside: the same person occupying the roles of member and arbiter, simultaneously. And for anyone wondering what happens to community members who press the question anyway, this is where my own small part in the story comes in.

Earlier this year, when criticism of Amanda was at its loudest, I posted a video defending her. My position was, and remains, that she deserves to be safe no matter what she does or says. But in a comment, I added the one reservation I couldn't leave out. I wrote that I didn't like the way she represents autism, because it was giving aspie supremacy, and in the same breath, that this didn't negate her right to be safe. Some weeks later, on a live stream, Amanda described what happened next, and I'd rather let her tell it. The he in that clip is me. And I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make too much of this, and easy to make too little. On a personal level, a celebrity blocking a commenter is nothing. It happens thousands of times a day, and nobody owes anybody a debate. But notice what actually occurred. A supporter, an autistic supporter, raised a concern using the community's own vocabulary, a term coined by a non-speaking autistic writer as a warning to protect the most vulnerable. And the response was not disagreement, or discussion, or even dismissal. It was removal. Which is, if you think about it, the member-and-arbiter dynamic performing itself in miniature. The question wasn't answered. The questioner was subtracted.

If everything this video has described is real, then the theory ought to make a prediction. A fingerprint we should expect to find. I've come to think of it as the support-claim contradiction, and it goes like this. When status is on the table, the discourse claims exceptional capacity: genius, talent, a higher IQ, a mind too well-wired to be indoctrinated. But when accountability is on the table, the discourse reaches the other way, toward incapacity, and draws on the understanding and accommodation owed to it. Both claims, from the same person, about the same trait, switching with the situation. And in Amanda's streams, you can watch the switch operate almost in real time. In one stream, when viewers say she's yelling, the explanation is that she simply speaks loudly, and that this is part of her autism. In another stream, weeks later, the same explanation is explicitly revoked: this time, she says, she isn't yelling because of her autism. She's yelling because her audience is annoying. Now, I want to be precise about what I am and am not saying, because this is the part most easily twisted. Speaking loudly, taking things literally, being overwhelmed by a wall of capital letters in a chat: these are real autistic experiences. They're mine, too. Claiming them is not the problem. The pattern is the problem: capacity, whenever status is being claimed, and incapacity, whenever accountability is being avoided, with the switch under the control of the person it protects. A community can extend understanding, or it can extend admiration. What it cannot coherently do is extend both, on demand, to the same behavior, forever. And when it's asked to, something in the community begins to wear out.

So who actually pays for all of this? If this were just a celebrity being messy on the internet, it wouldn't be worth an hour of your time, or the months this took to research. But listen to the people in and around her own audience, many of them autistic themselves, and a cost begins to come into focus. One creator, herself diagnosed, put it about as plainly as it can be put: weaponizing autism to allow harm without consequences is dangerous, and you can be autistic and still be accountable, because intention doesn't negate impact. Another asked the question that lingers over everything: if we sometimes miss social cues because we're autistic, how is it that everyone else is always the one misreading us? These are not attacks from outside the community. This is the community, trying to have the conversation the research has been inviting for two decades. And beyond the people willing to speak, count the people who simply disappear when autism gets re-centered on genius. Non-speaking autistic people, the very people Mel Baggs spent their life defending, vanish from the frame entirely. High-support-needs autistic people, and the families who care for them, vanish with them. Every autistic person whose autism cannot be performed, monetized, or turned into content is quietly informed that theirs is the lesser kind. And there's one more cost, harder to measure. Every time the world watches the toggle flip in public, it trusts the next self-identified autistic person a little less. The people with the least power end up paying the bill run up by the person with the most. Which is, if you remember, exactly what Táíwò's theory of elite capture predicts, and exactly what Mel Baggs was warning about in 2010.

So, how do we talk about Amanda Seales? I've spent this whole video trying to earn an honest answer to that question, and I think it's this: carefully, in both directions. Amanda Seales did not invent one piece of the machinery we've traced. The hierarchy of impairment predates her. Aspie supremacy predates her. The open door of self-identification, with all its justice and all its vulnerability, predates her. If she disappeared from public life tomorrow, the same pattern would find another face, because the mechanism doesn't depend on her. In that sense, she isn't the disease. She's a symptom the model predicts. And that's precisely why the answer to all of this cannot be hating her. The comment that got me blocked said that she deserves to be safe no matter what, and I meant it then and I mean it now: safe from harassment, from racism, from the endless bloodlust of the internet. But safety and accountability were never opposites. A healthy community is the place where you can receive both, protection and correction, sometimes in the same breath. Loving correction, you might even call it. Autism belongs to all of us. The speaking and the non-speaking. The geniuses and the gloriously ordinary. The clinically diagnosed, the self-identified, and the people still working up the courage to ask the question. No one gets to own the word, no matter how much we may love them. And if the research of the last twenty years teaches anything, it's that the moment someone starts ranking us, everyone below the line pays. The least we can do is notice while it's happening, and say so, out loud, with care.
