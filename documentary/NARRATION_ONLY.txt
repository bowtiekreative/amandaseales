Everything I've shown you has been documented by researchers — piece by piece — for over twenty years.
Two thousand three. Mark Deal, in the journal Disability and Society, documents what he calls a hierarchy of impairments. Disabled people ranking other disabled people. Distancing themselves from the "wrong kind" of disabled. Competing for scarce resources and respectability.

Two thousand ten. An autistic blogger named Mel Baggs writes an essay with a title that should have ended this conversation forever: "Aspie supremacy can kill."
Baggs — a non-speaking autistic person — warned that when "smart aspies" separate themselves from the rest of us, the people left below the line pay the price.

Two thousand nineteen. Researcher Anna de Hooge, in Disability Studies Quarterly, publishes "Binary Boys: Autism, Aspie Supremacy and Post-Humanist Normativity."
De Hooge shows the aspie-supremacist archetype is always drawn the same way — superior because intelligent, superior because productive, superior because economically worthy.

Twenty twenty-five. Patrick Dwyer and colleagues, in the journal Autism, describe "neurodiversity-lite": performatively celebrating neurodivergence while stereotyping neurodivergent people as having marketable superpowers.
Superpowers. Remember "the supertism"?

Twenty twenty-two. Philosopher Olufemi Taiwo publishes "Elite Capture: How the Powerful Took Over Identity Politics."

Taiwo's argument, in one line: whenever a group holds something valuable in common — a movement, a language, an identity — the most advantaged members of that group tend to end up controlling it, and speaking in its name.
Not because they're villains. Because they have the capital — money, fame, platform, fluency — and nobody audits them.
