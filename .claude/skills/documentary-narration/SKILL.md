# Documentary Narration — the Digital Stem Cell house voice

Ryan Perez (@digitalstemcell) makes long-form video essays and documentaries about
neurodivergent life, culture, and media. Every film's narration is written in one
voice: the **measured retrospective essayist**. This skill defines that voice.

The single most important thing to understand: this voice **earns its conclusions
slowly**. It is not a manifesto, a takedown, or a hype reel. It is a patient essay,
read aloud, by someone who did the reading and wants you to see what they saw. The
viewer should feel guided, never pushed. If a line would sound at home in a movie
trailer, it does not belong in the script.

Read `references/style-guide.md` before writing — it contains annotated excerpts
from the exemplar and real before/after pairs showing the voice done wrong and
then done right. The rules below are the skeleton; the reference file is the ear.

## The stance

Write as a curious insider, not a prosecutor. The narrator has a point of view and
does reach conclusions — sometimes damning ones — but arrives at them through
evidence the viewer has just seen, and says them with regret rather than relish.
When the narrator has a personal stake in the story, it is disclosed early in one
modest sentence ("I should mention up front that I have a small part in this
story — we'll get to it later"), then held back until late in the film, and
underplayed when it arrives ("it would be easy to make too much of this, and easy
to make too little").

## Structure: context before judgment

Order the film so the viewer understands the subject before being asked to judge it:

1. **Significance first.** Open with why the subject matters — accomplishments,
   scale, cultural weight — and end the intro by naming the question the film asks.
   Never open with the grievance or the verdict.
2. **History next.** A chapter of background before any critique. If the film is
   about a person, show their career; if about a work, its era and origins.
3. **The good-faith chapter.** Before the turn, give the subject their best case,
   genuinely — not as a setup, but because it is true. ("It's worth sitting with
   how genuinely moving this is.") The critique lands harder because the credit
   was real.
4. **The turn, shown not asserted.** Introduce the problem through specific clips
   or scenes; describe what happens, play it, then unpack what it *does*.
5. **The research chapter.** Scholars and sources by full name, year, and
   publication, read into the narration as prose ("In 2003, researcher Mark Deal
   published a paper in the journal Disability and Society titled…").
6. **Personal material late.** The narrator's own story, if any, plays after the
   framework is built — the way the exemplar holds "What Bill Did" until near the
   end — so it reads as evidence, not motive.
7. **A reflective close.** Return to the title question and answer it with nuance
   ("carefully, in both directions"). Then a modest outro: this was hard to make,
   here's how to support the work, thank you for watching. The ask is soft and
   brief, never a pitch.

## Sentence craft

- Medium-to-long flowing sentences with subordinate clauses, connected by
  conversational transitions: "Of course," "In fact," "Ultimately," "Perhaps most
  striking is," "Which brings us to."
- **No staccato fragment chains.** "Blocked. Not a troll. Not an enemy." is the
  banned register. One-word dramatic sentences read as trailer copy.
- **No commands at the viewer.** Never "Sit with that." / "Watch the toggle
  operate." / "Let that sink in." Instead, invite: "It's worth pausing on what
  those three measures actually are." / "Consider the shape of that rule for a
  moment."
- Rhetorical questions are allowed but gentle and sparing, usually to open a
  section: "Which raises an obvious question: if the warnings have been on the
  record this whole time, why does the pattern keep happening?"
- First person appears regularly but modestly: "I appreciate how…", "What's
  frustrating about this plotline is…", "I've come to think of it as…". Never
  swaggering ("with my whole chest" is exactly the register to avoid).

## The signature moves

Use these where they fit — they are what makes the voice recognizable:

- **The both-directions caveat:** "That's not to say X. Really bad things never
  happen to the Huxtable kids — bad things only ever happen *around* them."
  State the limit of your own claim before a critic does.
- **The precision disclaimer** before the sharpest critique: "I want to be
  precise about what I am and am not saying, because this is the part most
  easily twisted."
- **Credit-then-complication:** "This was a really great episode… But much like
  other moments in the series, it presents this struggle as historic."
- **The pattern reveal:** show two or three specific instances, then name the
  pattern they share. Evidence first, label second — never the reverse.
- **Read the scholar aloud:** quote critics and researchers in full sentences
  attributed by name and outlet, letting them carry the strongest judgments so
  the narrator doesn't have to.

## Fairness is load-bearing

Concede everything true on the subject's side, plainly and early: "On all of
this, she is simply right, and it needs to be said plainly." Distinguish opinion
from fact when it matters. When the subject has answered a criticism, include
their answer. This is not politeness — it is what makes the sharp conclusions
credible, and it is what separates this voice from a takedown channel.

## Formatting for AI voiceover

The deliverable is usually fed to an AI voice, so:

- Spoken words only — no headers, stage directions, or markdown in the final
  narration file. Separate blocks with blank lines (each blank line is a safe
  split point and a chapter boundary unless told otherwise).
- Punctuate for breath: commas where the voice should pause, periods over
  semicolons.
- Make everything speakable: "two thousand three" rhythm is achieved by writing
  years as words when they open a sentence; strip diacritics that break TTS
  (write "Taiwo," note the pronunciation for the editor separately); avoid
  slashes, parentheses and symbols the voice would mangle.
- If the film has a clip structure, keep narration blocks in the same order as
  the cut sheet and note any clip whose position changed.

## Length calibration

Narration usually carries a third to a half of total runtime, with clips carrying
the rest. At a conversational TTS pace of roughly 150 words per minute: a
60-minute film wants about 3,000–4,500 narration words; a 20-minute essay about
1,500–2,200. Confirm the runtime target before writing, and say what the word
count implies for it when delivering.

## Before delivering, check the draft against this list

1. Does the intro establish significance and pose a question, rather than open
   with the verdict?
2. Is there a genuine good-faith passage before the first critique?
3. Any staccato fragments, trailer lines, or commands at the viewer? Rewrite them.
4. Is every named source given a full name, year, and publication, in prose?
5. Is the personal material disclosed early, delivered late, and underplayed?
6. Does the ending answer the film's question with nuance and close with a soft
   support ask and thanks?
7. Is the file clean spoken text an AI voice can read straight through?
