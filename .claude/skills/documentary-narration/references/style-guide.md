# The ear: annotated exemplars and before/after pairs

The house voice is modeled on long-form YouTube retrospectives — the canonical
exemplar is José's "How Do We Talk About The Cosby Show? A Retrospective"
(2021, ~1.8M views). This file contains excerpts from that exemplar with notes
on what each one is doing, followed by real before/after pairs from a Digital
Stem Cell film where the first draft missed the voice and the rewrite hit it.

## Exemplar excerpts, annotated

**The intro move — significance, then the question:**

> "The Cosby Show was one of the most successful sitcoms of all time… Though the
> revelation surrounding Bill Cosby's predatory behavior cast a shadow on the
> series, well before then, and certainly during its run, there was a deep
> critical contention about what to make of The Cosby Show's vision of the world…
> But before we dig into what the show means, we have to understand what it was,
> and the context in which it premiered."

Note: the scandal is acknowledged in one clause, then set aside. The intro's job
is scale and framing, and it ends by promising context before judgment.

**The pattern reveal — instances first, label second:**

> "That's not to say that these things don't exist in the world of The Cosby
> Show. Really bad things never happen to the Huxtable kids. Bad things only
> ever happen around them."

Note: this line lands only because three specific episodes were shown first
(the joint, the cigarettes, the friend's addiction). The generalization is the
*conclusion* of the sequence, not its headline. Also note the both-directions
caveat built into the first sentence.

**Credit-then-complication:**

> "This was a really great episode, maybe my favorite of the first 3 seasons…
> But much like other moments discussing racial discrimination in the series,
> it presents this struggle for racial equality as historic. Recent history,
> yes, but history nonetheless."

Note: the credit is real and specific ("maybe my favorite"), which is exactly
why the complication is trusted.

**Reading the scholar aloud:**

> "One limitation of that approach is described by sociologist Stuart Hall when
> he wrote: 'The problem with the positive/negative strategy is that adding
> positive images to the largely negative repertoire of the dominant regime of
> representation increases the diversity of ways in which being black is
> represented, but does not necessarily displace the negative.'"

Note: full name, discipline, and a complete quoted sentence. The scholar carries
the judgment; the narrator carries the scholar.

**Handling the ugliest material — flat, factual, unadorned:**

> "The following section includes sexual assault. If you would like to skip
> ahead, go to the time stamp below. In 2004, Andrea Constand, director of
> operations for the women's basketball team at Temple University, claimed…"

Note: when the material is at its most damning, the prose gets *plainer*. Dates,
names, filings. No intensifiers. The facts are allowed to be enough.

**The modest personal note and soft close:**

> "This video was very tough to make. Watching the 8 seasons of The Cosby Show
> wasn't even the toughest part… A lot of hard work went into making this video,
> so if you would like to see more of these in the future, please consider
> becoming a patron…"

Note: the ask arrives only after the work is done, framed as an invitation, and
the video ends on gratitude.

## Before / after: the same beats, wrong voice then right voice

These pairs are from a real Digital Stem Cell film. The "before" was rejected as
too punchy; the "after" was approved. Study the difference — it is the whole skill.

**Opening the film.**

Before (rejected):
> "Two weeks ago, I got blocked. Not by a troll. Not by an enemy. By someone I
> was defending… Stay with me for an hour. I'm going to show you a mechanism.
> And once you see it, you will see it everywhere."

After (approved):
> "Amanda Seales is one of the most recognizable multi-hyphenates of her
> generation. A comedian, an actress, a singer, a poet, an author… In 2024,
> sitting across from Shannon Sharpe on Club Shay Shay, she shared something
> that would reshape how a lot of people understood her: she was autistic…
> I should mention up front that I have a small personal part in this story.
> We'll get to it later, when it becomes relevant."

Why: the before opens on the narrator's grievance in trailer fragments and
commands the viewer. The after opens on the subject's significance, poses the
question, and discloses the personal stake in one modest sentence.

**Delivering the sharpest analytical point.**

Before (rejected):
> "Sit with the logic of that clip. 'You can't be good at that many things and
> not be autistic.' … it redefines the category — with talent as the entry
> ticket."

After (approved):
> "Now, being multi-talented is not a diagnostic criterion for autism. It never
> has been, and most autistic people are not celebrated multi-hyphenates, nor
> are they any less autistic for it. But notice what this argument does. It
> doesn't merely describe Amanda Seales. It quietly redefines what autism is,
> with exceptional ability as the proof of membership."

Why: same conclusion, but the command ("Sit with…") becomes an observation, the
fragment becomes a sentence, and the claim is cushioned by the both-directions
caveat about most autistic people.

**Handling the personal injury.**

Before (rejected):
> "The 'he' in that clip — is me. I'm the mushroom… The answer was not a
> question. Not a conversation. The answer was a block."

After (approved):
> "The he in that clip is me. And I want to be careful here, because it would be
> easy to make too much of this, and easy to make too little. On a personal
> level, a celebrity blocking a commenter is nothing. It happens thousands of
> times a day, and nobody owes anybody a debate. But notice what actually
> occurred… The question wasn't answered. The questioner was subtracted."

Why: the after deflates its own drama first, concedes the ordinary reading, and
only then makes the structural point — which is why the final line can afford to
be quiet.

**Ending the film.**

Before (rejected):
> "Critique is not attack. A block is not a rebuttal… I will say it in this film
> with my whole chest."

After (approved):
> "So, how do we talk about Amanda Seales? I've spent this whole video trying to
> earn an honest answer to that question, and I think it's this: carefully, in
> both directions… Safety and accountability were never opposites. A healthy
> community is the place where you can receive both, protection and correction,
> sometimes in the same breath."

Why: the after returns to the title question, answers it with nuance, and keeps
the temperature low all the way through the last line.

## Quick vocabulary of the voice

Transitions and framings that belong: "It's worth sitting with…", "Perhaps the
clearest example…", "Taken one clip at a time… Taken together…", "Which brings
us to…", "To be fair…", "That's not to say…", "I want to be careful here…",
"Reduced to a sentence, the argument goes like this…", "And that, I think, is
what makes the next part so difficult to see clearly."

Registers that do not belong: trailer fragments, all-caps energy, "let that sink
in", "full stop" as a mic-drop, "with my whole chest", second-person commands,
calling anything "insane"/"wild"/"unhinged", victory-lap phrasing over the
subject's failures.
